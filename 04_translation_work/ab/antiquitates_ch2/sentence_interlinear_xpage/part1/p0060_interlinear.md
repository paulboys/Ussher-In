**LA**  Idem etiam in Senensi concilio anno MCCCCXXIV.

**EN**  The same claim was also put forward at the Council of Siena, in the year 1424,

**LA**  a Richardo Flemmingo Lincolniensi episcopo et Lincolniensis collegii apud Oxonienses conditore, propositum est: quum adversus Hispanos, Scotos et Gallos coram Martino V. ab Anglis renovata esset hæc controversia. Sed in Basileensi præcipue concilio, anno MCCCCXXXIV. inter oratores regis Angliæ et Castellæ efferbuit hæc contentio : ubi quæ nostris hac in parte ab Alphonso Garsia de sancta Maria, legum doctore ac Compostellanæ et Segobiensis ecclesiarum decano, objecta sunt, ex schedis Cottonianis visum est proponere, et excutere. “Oratores regis Angliæ,” ait ille, “tam in præsentia vestrarum reverendissimarum paternitatum, quam in sacra deputatione reformatorii, dixerunt præferendos se propter antiquiorem susceptionem fidei Christianæ. Nam dicunt, quod ille nobilis decurio Joseph ab Arimathia una cum aliis, anno XV. post assumptionem gloriosæ virginis Mariæ venit in Angliam, et magnam ejus partem ad fidem Christi convertit.

**EN**  by Richard Fleming, bishop of Lincoln and founder of Lincoln College at Oxford, when this controversy had been renewed by the English against the Spaniards, the Scots, and the French before Martin V; but it was above all at the Council of Basel, in the year 1434, that this contention blazed up between the ambassadors of the kings of England and of Castile, and I have thought it fitting to set out from the Cotton papers, and to examine, the objections raised there against our side in this matter by Alfonso García de Santa María, doctor of laws and dean of the churches of Compostela and Segovia. "The ambassadors of the king of England," he says, "both in the presence of your most reverend paternities and in the sacred deputation on reform, have said that they ought to be given precedence on account of their more ancient reception of the Christian faith. For they say that the noble decurion Joseph of Arimathea, together with others, came into England in the fifteenth year after the assumption of the glorious Virgin Mary, and converted a great part of it to the faith of Christ.

**LA**  Et post non longo tempore a passione Christi, Eleutherius papa regnum Angliæ totaliter convertit ad fidem.”

**EN**  And not long after the passion of Christ, Pope Eleutherius converted the kingdom of England wholly to the faith."

**LA**  Ad primum vero illud de Josepho respondet. I. “Per interemptionem. Nego enim (inquit) Joseph illum venisse in Angliam. Ille ergo qui dicit, probat: (juxta l. Ei qui ff. de proba.) Producatur ergo in medium aliqua historia vel scriptura authentica. II. Per probabilitatem contrariam. Nam licet ei qui negat non incumbat probatio: (ut l. actor. C. de proba.) tamen interdum probatur negativa per judicium; quando habet comitantiam temporum. (ut l. optimam C. contrahentium et com. stipu.)

**EN**  To that first point about Joseph, however, he responds: I. "By destruction of the claim. For I deny (he says) that this Joseph came into England. He who asserts, therefore, must prove it (according to the law Ei qui, Digest, On Proofs): so let some authentic history or writing be brought forward. II. By contrary probability. For although the burden of proof does not rest on the one who denies (as in the law Actor, Code, On Proofs), nevertheless a negative is sometimes proved by judgment, when it has the concurrence of the times (as in the law Optimam, Code, On Contracting and Committing Stipulations).

**LA**  In hoc ergo casu bene potest probari hæc negativa per scripturam contrariam. Continetur enim in floribus sanctorum, sub legenda Jacobi minoris, contrarium. Hoc legitur quoque quod Titus intrans Hierusalem, quemdam murum densissimum vidit, ipsumque perforari jussit: et intus quendam senem repererunt, qui dixit se esse Joseph ab Arimathia, et se ibidem esse inclusum a Judæis quia Christum sepelisset, et quod usque ad tempus istud cœlesti cibo sit pastus. In evangelio tamen Nicodemi dicitur<sup id="fnref-p0060-t"><a href="#fn-seg_p0061_fn_001">t</a></sup>, quod Christus eum liberavit post resurrectionem suam, et in Arimathiam duxit. Potest autem dici quod eductus cum a prædicatione non cessaret, iterum sit ab ipsis Judæis inclusus. Hæc ubi supra. Cum ergo Joseph fuerit incarceratus tanto tempore apud Jerusalem, impossibile est eum fuisse in Anglia, cum sit magna distantia.

**EN**  In this case, therefore, this negative can well be proved by contrary writing: for the contrary is contained in the Flowers of the Saints, under the legend of James the Less, where it is also read that Titus, entering Jerusalem, saw a certain very thick wall and ordered it to be broken through; and inside they found an old man, who said that he was Joseph of Arimathea, and that he had been shut up there by the Jews because he had buried Christ, and that up to that time he had been fed with heavenly food. In the Gospel of Nicodemus, however, it is said<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0061_fn_001">t</a></sup> that Christ freed him after his resurrection and led him to Arimathea; but it may be said that, once brought out, since he did not cease from preaching, he was shut up again by those same Jews. So far the passage cited above. Since, then, Joseph was imprisoned for so long a time at Jerusalem, it is impossible that he was in England, the distance being so great.
