**LA**  Polydorus Vergilius, vir naris emunctioris, sublatis crassioribus illis monachorum fabulamentis, historiam hanc ita proponit. “ Joseph<sup id="fnref-p0054-t"><a href="#fn-seg_p0054_fn_002">t</a></sup> ille, qui, teste Matthæo evangelista, ab Arimathæa civitate oriundus, Christi corpus sepeliverat, sive casu, sive consilio, ita volente Deo, cum non parvo comitatu, in Britanniam venit: ubi tam ipse, quam ejus socii, cum de evangelio prædicarent, atque dogma Christi sedulo docerent, multi per hæc ad veram pietatem traducti, salutiferaque fruge imbuti, baptizati sunt. Isti viri certe divino spiritu aﬄati, cum a rege parum terræ ad inhabitandum (proxime Welliam oppidum, circiter millia passuum quatuor) dono accepissent, ibi novæ religionis prima jecerunt fundamenta, ubi hodie est templum magnificum cœnobiumque monasticæ familiæ D. Benedicti ordinis nobile. Loco nomen est Glasconia. Hæc omnino Christianæ pietatis in Britannia extitere primordia; quam deinde Lucius rex, sicut paulo post dicemus, prope extinctam baptismi fonte sublatus mirabiliter accendit, pieque adauxit. Nam Gildas testis est, Britannos jam inde ab initio orti evangelii, Christianam recepisse religionem."

**EN**  Polydore Vergil, a man of more discriminating judgement, having set aside those cruder fables of the monks, presents this history as follows: "That Joseph<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0054_fn_002">t</a></sup> who, as the evangelist Matthew attests, was a native of the city of Arimathea and had buried the body of Christ, came to Britain — whether by chance or by design, God so willing — with a considerable company; and there, as both he and his companions preached the gospel and diligently taught the doctrine of Christ, many were by these means brought over to true piety, imbued with the fruit that brings salvation, and baptized. These men, assuredly filled with the divine Spirit, when they had received from the king as a gift a small parcel of land to dwell in (near the town of Wells, at a distance of about four miles), there laid the first foundations of the new religion, where today stands a magnificent church and a noble monastery of the monastic family of the order of St. Benedict. The name of the place is Glastonbury. These were altogether the beginnings of Christian piety in Britain, which — when it was nearly extinguished — King Lucius, as I shall relate a little later, having been raised from the font of baptism, wonderfully rekindled and piously enlarged. For Gildas is witness that the Britons received the Christian religion from the very first rising of the gospel."
