## Interlinear

*⟵ The opening lines of this page complete the final sentence of p0054 (seg_p0054_s0001), translated there. This page's own text begins below at l0015.*

**LA**  Hæc Polydorus. Et ante eum Richardus Lepidus Bartholinus. "Nec<sup id="fnref-p0055-x"><a href="#fn-seg_p0055_fn_001">x</a></sup> me præterit," inquit, "Josephum ab Arimathea, cum a Judæis nequiter in carcerem ductus fuisset, et a Deo Optimo Maximo miraculose liberatum taliter ut nusquam postea in Judæa visum fuisse. Nam, ut annalium scriptores prodiderunt, in Britanniam transmigravit; ibique et cultum Christi docuit, et multa sui monumenta in valle Avilonis, quæ adhuc visuntur, reliquit."

**EN**  So far Polydore; and before him Richardus Lepidus Bartholinus. "Nor<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0055_fn_001">x</a></sup> has it escaped me," he says, "that Joseph of Arimathea, after he had been wickedly thrown into prison by the Jews, was so miraculously delivered by Almighty God that he was never afterward seen in Judaea; for, as the writers of annals have recorded, he crossed over into Britain, and there he both taught the worship of Christ and left many memorials of himself in the vale of Avalon, which are still to be seen."

**LA**  Eadem etiam de Josephi adventu refert Thomas Scropus, Dromorensis quondam in Hibernia episcopus, capite septimo libri quem de Carmelitani ordinis antiquitate scripsit. Et Thomas Harfeldius, in breviario regum Anglorum: ubi Faganum et Duvianum (sive Deruvianum) Lucii regis temporibus in insula Avalloniæ "antiquam ecclesiam a discipulis Christi fabricatam reperisse dicit, cum antiquis scriptis, qualiter sanctis apostolis ad prædicandum dispersis, sanctus Philippus in Franciam veniens duodecim ex suis discipulis in Britanniam misit; quibus et Joseph ab Arimathia præfecit." Et extat sane adhuc magni nostri Patricii nomine, a Glastoniensibus monachis conficta epistola (charta ea est B. Patricii, a Malmesburiensi superius citata) in qua de origine ecclesiæ quæ Anglo-Saxonibus Ealdcirc, id est, vetusta ecclesia dicta est, ita ille disserens introducitur. “Ostenderunt mihi fratres scripta sanctorum Phagani et Deruviani: in quibus continebatur, quod duodecim discipuli sanctorum Philippi et Jacobi ipsam vetustam ecclesiam construxerant in honore prælibatæ advocatricis nostræ, B. Mariæ virginis, per doctrinamentum beati archangeli Gabrielis. Insuper et quod Dominus eandem ecclesiam cœlitus in honore suæ matris dedicaverat: et quod tres reges pagani ipsis duodecim, ad eorum sustenementum, duodecim portiones terræ dederunt.” Indeque Malmesburiensis: “ Duodecim discipulis sanctorum Philippi et Jacobi anno Dominicæ Incarnationis LXIII. in Britanniam venientibus, tres reges licet pagani XII. portiones terræ contulerunt: unde adhuc nomen XII. hidarum perseverat.” Hæc ille in libro de antiquitate Glastoniensis ecclesiæ: ubi XII. harum hidarum metas cum locis præcipuis quæ intra earum limites continentur, diligenter describit. Earundem etiam in libro censuali Guilielmi I. Anglorum regis, qui Domesday<sup id="fnref-p0055-y"><a href="#fn-seg_p0056_fn_001">y</a></sup> appellatur, ejusmodi fit mentio. “ Ecclesia Glastingebury habet in ipsa villa XII. hydas quæ nunquam geldaverunt.”

**EN**  The same account of Joseph's coming is also given by Thomas Scrope, sometime bishop of Dromore in Ireland, in the seventh chapter of the book which he wrote on the antiquity of the Carmelite order; and by Thomas Harfeldius, in his compendium of the kings of the English, where he says that Fagan and Duvian (or Deruvian), in the days of King Lucius, "found in the isle of Avalon an ancient church built by the disciples of Christ, together with ancient writings telling how, when the holy apostles were dispersed to preach, Saint Philip, coming into France, sent twelve of his disciples into Britain, and set Joseph of Arimathea over them." And there is indeed still extant, under the name of our great Patrick, a letter forged by the monks of Glastonbury (this is the charter of St. Patrick cited above from William of Malmesbury), in which he is introduced discoursing as follows on the origin of the church which the Anglo-Saxons called Ealdcirc, that is, "the old church": "The brothers showed me the writings of Saints Phaganus and Deruvianus, in which it was contained that twelve disciples of Saints Philip and James had built that old church in honour of our aforesaid advocate, the Blessed Virgin Mary, at the instruction of the blessed archangel Gabriel; and further, that the Lord had dedicated that same church from heaven in honour of his mother, and that three pagan kings gave to those twelve, for their sustenance, twelve portions of land." And hence William of Malmesbury: "When the twelve disciples of Saints Philip and James came into Britain in the sixty-third year of the Lord's Incarnation, three kings, though pagans, conferred upon them twelve portions of land; whence the name of the Twelve Hides persists to this day." So he (William of Malmesbury) writes in his book "On the Antiquity of the Church of Glastonbury," where he carefully describes the bounds of these twelve hides, together with the principal places contained within their limits; and mention of the same is likewise made in the survey-book of William I, king of the English, which is called Domesday<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0056_fn_001">y</a></sup>, in these terms: "The church of Glastonbury has in that same vill twelve hides which have never paid geld."
