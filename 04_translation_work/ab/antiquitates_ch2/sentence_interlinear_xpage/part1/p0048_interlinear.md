**LA**  Addit præterea Malmesburiensis, ad Glastoniensis ecclesiæ antiquitatem comprobandam, et sequens testimonium: “Monachus quidam Glastoniæ, Godefridus nomine, (de cujus epistola hoc capitulum assumpsimus) tempore Henrici Blesensis, abbatis Glastoniensis, cum in pago Parisiacensi apud sanctum Dionysium moraretur; senior quidam ex monachis interrogavit eum: quo genus, unde domo? Respondit, Normannum, e Britanniæ monasterio quod Glastingeia dicitur, monachum. Papæ, inquit, an adhuc stat illa perpetuæ virginis et misericordiæ matris vetusta ecclesia? Stat, inquit. Tum ille lepido attactu caput G. Glastoniensis demulcens, diu silentio suspensum tenuit, ac sic demum ora resolvit; hæc gloriosissimi martyris Dionysii ecclesia, et illa de qua te asseris, eandem privilegii dignitatem habent: ista in Gallia, illa in Britannia uno eodem tempore exortæ, a summo et magno pontifice consecratæ. Uno tamen gradu illa supereminet: Roma etenim secunda vocatur.”

**EN**  Malmesbury adds, moreover, in order to confirm the antiquity of the church of Glastonbury, this further testimony: “A certain monk of Glastonbury, Godfrey by name (from whose letter I have taken this chapter), in the time of Henry of Blois, abbot of Glastonbury, was staying in the district of Paris at Saint-Denis, when one of the elder monks asked him of what people he was and where his home lay. He answered that he was a Norman, a monk of the monastery in Britain which is called Glastingeia. ‘Marvellous!’ said the elder; ‘does that ancient church of the perpetual Virgin and Mother of mercy still stand?’ ‘It stands,’ he replied. Then the elder, stroking the head of G. of Glastonbury with a playful touch, held him long in suspense in silence, and at last opened his lips thus: ‘This church of the most glorious martyr Denis and that one to which you claim to belong possess the same dignity of privilege: the one in Gaul, the other in Britain, arose at one and the same time and were consecrated by the supreme and great pontiff. Yet in one degree the latter stands higher: for it is called a second Rome.’”

**LA**  Refert Cæsar Baronius ex<sup id="fnref-p0048-b"><a href="#fn-seg_p0048_fn_001">b</a></sup> actis Mariæ Magdalenæ et sociorum, dispersione facta post mortem Stephani, “Lazarum, Mariam Magdalenam, Martham, et Marcellam pedissequam, in quos Judæi majori odio exardescebant, non tantum Hierosolymis pulsos esse, sed una cum Maximino discipulo, navi absque remigio impositos, in certum periculum mari fuisse creditos: quos divina providentia Massiliam tradunt appulisse.” Additque ex historia Anglorum, quæ manuscripta habetur in bibliotheca Vaticana, sed (quod ille tacet, et nos suo loco patefaciemus) nostris temporibus scripta, “comitem ejusdem discriminis Josephum ab Arimathæa nobilem decurionem; quem tradunt ex Gallia in Britanniam navigasse, illicque post prædicatum evangelium diem clausisse extremum.” In PseudoDextri quoque chronico, ad annum Christi XLVIII. similiter legimus; “ Hierosolymitani Judæi vehementer infensi B. Lazaro, Magdalenæ, Marthæ, Marcellæ, Maximino, Josepho ab Arimathia nobili decurioni, et aliis plurimis ; navi sine remigio velisque, ac sine gubernatore eos imponunt, et exulare mandant : qui per vastum mare divinitus delati, ad Massiliensem portum incolumes appellunt.” Et in fragmento Helecæ attributo; “Fama est, Magdalenam Lazari sororem, cum Lazaro, Maximo atque Chelidonio, Marcella, Josepho ab Arimathia, venisse in Galliam Aquitanicam; ibique prædicasse Domini Jesu sanctum evangelium: ut docent luculenter Gallorum historiæ, et est traditio ubique satis recepta illius regionis.” Quod autem de Philippi in Galliis apostolatu habet Freculphus a Malmesburiensi citatus; ex Isidori libro de patribus utriusque Testamenti<sup id="fnref-p0048-c"><a href="#fn-seg_p0049_fn_001">c</a></sup> ad verbum expressit. Apud utrumque enim legimus, Philippum Gallis prædicasse Christum, “ barbarasque gentes, vicinasque tenebris, et tumenti oceano conjunctas, ad scientiæ lucem, fideique portum deduxisse.” Neque hic mihi Baronii<sup id="fnref-p0048-d"><a href="#fn-seg_p0049_fn_002">d</a></sup> conjectura placet, Isidori ista a Gallis nostris ad Galatas Asiaticos traducentis : multoque minus, nuperi operum Isidori editoris, Jacobi Breulii temeritas, Galatis pro Gallis in ipso textu, sine ulla veteris lectionis commemoratione, reponentis.

**EN**  Caesar Baronius relates, from<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0048_fn_001">b</a></sup> the acts of Mary Magdalene and her companions, that when the dispersion took place after the death of Stephen, “Lazarus, Mary Magdalene, Martha, and Marcella her attendant, against whom the Jews burned with the greater hatred, were not only driven out of Jerusalem but, together with the disciple Maximinus, were set on a ship without oars and committed to the sea to certain peril; and these, they relate, by divine providence landed at Marseilles.” And he adds, from a history of the English which is held in manuscript in the Vatican library, but which (a point he passes over in silence, and which I shall make plain in its proper place) was written in our own times, that “Joseph of Arimathea, the noble decurion, was a companion in the same peril; and they relate that he sailed from Gaul into Britain, and there, after preaching the gospel, ended his last day.” In the chronicle of Pseudo-Dexter likewise, at the year of Christ 48, I read to the same effect: “The Jews of Jerusalem, violently hostile to blessed Lazarus, Magdalene, Martha, Marcella, Maximinus, Joseph of Arimathea the noble decurion, and very many others, set them on a ship without oars or sails and without a pilot, and order them into exile; and these, borne by divine power across the vast sea, put in unharmed at the port of Marseilles.” And in the fragment attributed to Heleca: “The report is that Magdalene, the sister of Lazarus, came with Lazarus, Maximus and Chelidonius, Marcella, and Joseph of Arimathea into Aquitanian Gaul, and there preached the holy gospel of the Lord Jesus: as the histories of the Gauls plainly teach, and it is a tradition quite generally received throughout that region.” But what Freculphus, cited by Malmesbury, has concerning Philip's apostolate in the Gauls, he copied word for word from Isidore's book “On the Fathers of Both Testaments”<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0049_fn_001">c</a></sup>; for in both I read that Philip preached Christ to the Gauls, and “brought the barbarous peoples, neighbors to darkness and bordering upon the swelling ocean, to the light of knowledge and the harbor of faith.” Nor here am I persuaded by the conjecture of Baronius<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0049_fn_002">d</a></sup>, who transfers these words of Isidore from our Gauls to the Asiatic Galatians; and much less by the rashness of Jacques du Breul, the recent editor of Isidore's works, who substituted ‘Galatians’ for ‘Gauls’ in the text itself, without any mention of the older reading.
