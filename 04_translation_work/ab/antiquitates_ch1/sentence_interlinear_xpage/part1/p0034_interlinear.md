## Reading

Where ships can reach, can the words of God not reach?”

And indeed Britain received the Gospel from its very first dawning. To this island—stiff with glacial cold and lying, as it were, in some remoter recess of the world, far from the visible sun—that true Sun first granted his rays in time: a Sun shining not from the temporal firmament alone, but from the highest citadel of the heavens that transcends all ages, displaying to the whole world the radiant gleam of himself. This he did, as we know, at the close of the reign of Tiberius Caesar, when Christ's religion was being spread without any hindrance—the senate unwilling, yet the emperor threatening death against the informers who denounced its followers. That is to say, Christ first granted his precepts. And although the inhabitants received these only tepidly, yet among some entirely and among others less fully, they endured until the persecution of Diocletian.”

So writes Gildas; and what he says here about the close of Tiberius's reign must be explained from Eusebius's chronicle, in which, at either the last or the next-to-last year of Tiberius, this is recorded: “When Pilate had reported to Tiberius concerning the doctrine of the Christians, Tiberius referred the matter to the senate, proposing that it be received among the other sacred rites. But when, by the deliberation of the senators, it had been resolved that the Christians should be expelled from the city, Tiberius by edict threatened death to the accusers of the Christians.
