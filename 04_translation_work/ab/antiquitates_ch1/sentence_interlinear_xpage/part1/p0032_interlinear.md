**LA**  PRISCÆ illius ignorantiæ<sup id="fnref-p0032-a"><a href="#fn-seg_p0032_fn_001">a</a></sup> temporibus, quæ salutarem Christi adventum præcesserunt, Britannos et Hibernos nostros communem reliquarum gentium sortem subiisse, id est, “fuisse<sup id="fnref-p0032-b"><a href="#fn-seg_p0032_fn_006">b</a></sup> absque Christo, alienos a republica Israelis, et extraneos quod ad fœdera promissionis attinet, spem non habentes, et sine Deo in mundo:” non est quod quisquam dubitet. “Neque enim Britannia fertilis provincia tyrannorum,” inquiebat olim impius Porphyrius<sup id="fnref-p0032-c"><a href="#fn-seg_p0032_fn_002">c</a></sup>, “et Scoticæ gentes omnesque usque ad oceanum per circuitum barbaræ nationes Moysen prophetasque cognoverant.” Neque minus falluntur viri<sup id="fnref-p0032-d"><a href="#fn-seg_p0032_fn_003">d</a></sup> docti, qui Britannos a Druydibus suis in unius Dei cultu semper institutos fuisse tradunt, quam nugatus est Postellus, quum ab Hebræis<sup id="fnref-p0032-e">e</sup> Hibernorum originem conatus est arcessere. Nam et Origenes<sup id="fnref-p0032-f">f</sup>, quem illi authorem citant, plane contrarium indicat, “terram Britanniæ ante adventum Christi in unius dei religionem” minime consensisse: nominatim vero Andrasten sive Andaten, Victoriæ deam, Dio Cassius<sup id="fnref-p0032-g">g</sup>, Apollinem et Dianam alii a Britannis coli consuevisse prodiderunt: et de eorum idolomania gentilis ipsorum Gildas tam clarum perhibet testimonium, ut res in quæstionem vocari non debeat. Sic enim ille: “Igitur omittens priscos illos communesquc cum omnibus gentibus errores, quibus ante adventum Christi in carne omne humanum genus obligabatur adstrictum; nec enumerans patriæ portenta ipsa diabolica, pene numero Egyptiaca vincentia, quorum nonnulla lineamentis adhuc deformibus intra vel extra deserta mœnia, solito more rigentia torvis vultibus intuemur: neque nominatim inclamitans montes ipsos, aut colles, vel fluvios, olim exitiales, nunc vero humanis usibus utiles, quibus divinus honor a cæco tunc populo cumulabatur, &c.” Ut de illorum temporum miserrima, et subsequentium felicissima conditione, in epitaphio Nepotiani B. Hieronymus pronuntiare non dubitaverit. “Ubi<sup id="fnref-p0032-h">h</sup> tunc totius orbis homines, ab India usque ad Britanniam, a rigida septentrionis plaga usque ad fervores Atlantici oceani tam innumerabiles populi, et tantarum gentium multitudines; Quam<sup id="fnref-p0032-i">i</sup> variæ linguis, habitu tam vestis et armis?

**EN**  No one need doubt that, in those times of ancient ignorance which preceded the saving advent of Christ, our Britons and Irish underwent the common lot of the other nations — that is, that they were “without Christ, aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers as regards the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world.” For, as the impious Porphyry once said, “neither had Britain, a province fertile in tyrants, nor the Scottish peoples and all the barbarous nations round about as far as the ocean, known Moses and the prophets.” Nor are the learned men who maintain that the Britons were always instructed by their Druids in the worship of one God any less mistaken than Postel was when he trifled in attempting to derive the origin of the Irish from the Hebrews. For even Origen, whom they cite as their authority, indicates plainly the contrary — that “the land of Britain, before the advent of Christ, by no means agreed in the religion of one God”; and specifically Dio Cassius has recorded that Andraste, or Andate, the goddess of Victory, and others that Apollo and Diana, were customarily worshipped by the Britons; and concerning their idol-madness their own countryman Gildas furnishes so clear a testimony that the matter ought not to be called into question. For thus he writes: “Therefore, passing over those ancient errors, common to all the nations, by which the whole human race was bound fast before the advent of Christ in the flesh; and not enumerating the diabolical monstrosities of my country, almost exceeding in number those of Egypt, some of which we still behold, with their features yet misshapen, within or without the deserted walls, rigid after the usual manner with their grim countenances; nor crying out by name against the very mountains, or hills, or rivers, once deadly but now useful for human purposes, upon which divine honour was then heaped by a blind people, &c.” So that the blessed Jerome, in his epitaph of Nepotian, did not hesitate to pronounce upon the most wretched condition of those times and the most happy condition of those that followed: “Where then were the men of the whole world, from India even to Britain, from the rigid region of the north even to the heats of the Atlantic ocean — peoples so innumerable, and multitudes of nations so great; how varied in their tongues, how varied in the fashion of their dress and their arms?”

## Reading

THE ANTIQUITIES OF THE BRITISH CHURCHES.

CHAPTER I. The Britons idolaters before they received the Christian faith. Concerning the apostles who are said to have first preached the gospel in the British Isles: James the son of Zebedee, Simon the Zealot, Simon Peter, and the blessed Paul. Likewise concerning Aristobulus and Claudia the British woman.

No one need doubt that, in those times of ancient ignorance which preceded the saving advent of Christ, our Britons and Irish shared the common lot of the other nations — that they were "without Christ, aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world." For, as the impious Porphyry once said, "neither Britain, a province fertile in tyrants, nor the Scottish peoples, nor any of the barbarous nations round about as far as the ocean, had known Moses and the prophets." Nor are the learned men who maintain that the Britons were always instructed by their Druids in the worship of one God any less mistaken than Postel was when he trifled in trying to derive the origin of the Irish from the Hebrews. For even Origen, whom they cite as their authority, plainly indicates the contrary — that "the land of Britain, before the coming of Christ, by no means agreed in the religion of one God." Indeed, Dio Cassius has specifically recorded that the Britons were accustomed to worship Andraste, or Andate, the goddess of Victory, and others report that they worshipped Apollo and Diana; and concerning their idol-madness their own countryman Gildas furnishes so clear a testimony that the matter ought not to be called into question.

For thus he writes: "Therefore I pass over those ancient errors, common to all the nations, by which the whole human race was held fast bound before the coming of Christ in the flesh; nor do I enumerate the diabolical monstrosities of my country, almost outdoing in number those of Egypt, some of which we still behold — their features yet misshapen, within or without the deserted walls, rigid in the usual manner with their grim countenances; nor do I cry out by name against the very mountains, or hills, or rivers, once deadly but now useful for human purposes, on which a people then blind heaped divine honour, and so forth."

So it was that the blessed Jerome, in his epitaph on Nepotian, did not hesitate to pronounce upon the most wretched condition of those times and the most happy condition of those that followed: "Where then were the men of the whole world, from India even to Britain, from the rigid region of the north even to the heats of the Atlantic ocean — peoples so innumerable, multitudes of nations so great; how varied in their tongues, how varied in the fashion of their dress and their arms?"
