**EN**  Jerome affirms that St. Paul, the teacher of the Gentiles, τὸν<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0040_fn_001">t</a></sup> τὴν οἰκουμένην σταδεύσαντα, καὶ τῷ περὶ πίστεως δρόμῳ τὸν κόσμον μικρὸν ἀποφήναντα [who traversed the inhabited world, and by his race for the faith made the world seem small] (as Basil of Seleucia speaks of him), "ran from ocean to ocean," "imitating his Lord<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0040_fn_002">u</a></sup> and the sun of righteousness, of whom we read: His going forth<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0040_fn_003">w</a></sup> is from the highest heaven, and his circuit reaches to the highest part of it, so that the earth would sooner fail than his zeal for preaching"; and that, after his first defense — which he himself mentions in the later epistle to Timothy — having been released by Nero, he preached the gospel<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0040_fn_004">x</a></sup> of Christ in the regions of the West as well. The words of the apostle are<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0040_fn_005">y</a></sup>: "At my first defense no one stood with me, but all forsook me. May it not be charged against them. But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me, so that through me the preaching might be fulfilled, and all the Gentiles might hear." On which passage Theodoret says: "When," he says, "having made his appeal, he was sent from Festus to Rome, his defense was heard and he was acquitted, and he set out for Spain, and, running out to other peoples, brought them the light of doctrine"; and, as the same author adds elsewhere<sup><a href="#fn-seg_p0040_fn_006">z</a></sup>, "He brought benefit to the islands that lie in the sea." ## Reading

Jerome affirms that St. Paul, the teacher of the Gentiles—τὸν τὴν οἰκουμένην σταδεύσαντα, καὶ τῷ περὶ πίστεως δρόμῳ τὸν κόσμον μικρὸν ἀποφήναντα (who traversed the inhabited world, and by his race for the faith made the world seem small), as Basil of Seleucia describes him—"ran from ocean to ocean," imitating his Lord, the sun of righteousness, of whom we read: "His going forth is from the highest heaven, and his circuit reaches to its farthest point," so that the earth would sooner give out than his zeal for preaching. Jerome adds that, after his first defense—which Paul himself mentions in the later epistle to Timothy—and after being released by Nero, he preached the gospel of Christ in the regions of the West as well. The apostle's own words are these: "At my first defense no one stood with me, but everyone abandoned me. May it not be charged against them. Yet the Lord stood by me and gave me strength, so that through me the preaching might be fulfilled and all the Gentiles might hear."

On this passage Theodoret comments: "When he had made his appeal and was sent from Festus to Rome, his defense was heard and he was acquitted; he then set out for Spain, and, going out to other peoples, brought them the light of doctrine." And, as the same author adds elsewhere, "He brought benefit to the islands that lie in the sea." It is for this reason that Venantius Fortunatus says of him: He crossed the Ocean, even where the island opens a harbor, and the lands the Briton holds, and those of farthest Thule.

Sophronius, patriarch of Jerusalem, in his sermon on the feast of the apostles, likewise indicates that Paul preached the gospel to the Spaniards and the Britons—though this I report on the testimony of others, since I have not yet managed to see the source myself. The old translator of Chrysostom's sermon on Pentecost also hints that Paul ran from Spain "to the farthest parts" of the earth. Finally, "like some heavenly bird ranging round the whole habitable world, his evangelical contest for Christ accomplished over thirty-five years," as Nicephorus writes, he completed his course at last by martyrdom at Rome under Nero.
