**LA**  faciat. Et quo Pudentem maritum, centurionem aut pri mipilarem in Britannia vel Germania militantem, exulem in Scotiam, ob Christianæ pietatis professionem, extra Ro mani imperii fines, relegatum fuisse colligit, ex illo Marti alis carmine : Cernere Parrhasios dum te juvat, Aule, triones Cominus et Scythici sidera ferre poli.

**EN**  ...he makes such mention. And from that poem of Martial he gathers that Pudens the husband — a centurion, or primipilaris, serving in Britain or Germany — was banished as an exile into Scotland, beyond the bounds of the Roman empire, on account of his profession of Christian piety: "While it delights you, Aulus, to gaze close at hand upon the Parrhasian Bears and to bear the stars of the Scythian pole."

**LA**  Ubi argutissimus criticus Scotici vocabulum, pro Scythici, reponendum esse conjicit: oblitus, apud Claudianum, Apollinarem Sidonium ac veteres illos, primam Scotici nominis syllabam semper productam legi; et in antiquis manuscriptis Scottos geminata litera passim exarari con suevisse. Neque sui dissimilis est, quum addit, Perso nium “probare in conversione Anglicana Claudiam non potuisse esse Anglam,” quasi quisquam eam Dempsteri insaniret insaniam, ut Claudiæ temporibus Angliam quæ reret in Britannia: aut id argumentatione sua conficere non conaretur Personius, B. Claudiam cujus Paulus meminit, non fuisse Britannam illam, cujus formæ decus Trajano imperante Martialis laudaverit; et consequenter, absque ullo fundamento vel Scotam constitui vel Anglam. Quan quam argumentum illud, quo potissimum ille nititur, a temporis ratione deductum, non multum urgeat. Neque enim si in undecimo quem citavimus epigrammatum libello occurrrunt aliqua sub tertio vel Nervæ vel Trajani consu latu conscripta carmina; sequetur, et reliqua omnia eodem tempore fuisse composita. Et licet sub Trajano mortem poeta obierit: senem tamen decessisse cogitandum est, et prius familiariter usum esse Silio Italico, secundi belli

**EN**  Here that most ingenious critic conjectures that the word Scotici ought to be substituted for Scythici — forgetting that in Claudian, Sidonius Apollinaris, and those ancient writers, the first syllable of the name Scotici is always read as long, and that in old manuscripts Scotti was commonly everywhere written with a doubled letter. Nor is he unlike himself when he adds that Persons "proves, in his treatise on the English conversion, that Claudia could not have been English" — as though anyone would rave with Dempster's madness so far as to look for England in Britain in Claudia's day, or as though Persons were not striving to establish by his argument that the blessed Claudia whom Paul mentions was not that British woman whose beauty Martial praised under the reign of Trajan, and that consequently she is set up, without any foundation, as either a Scot or an Englishwoman. And yet that argument on which he chiefly relies, drawn from the reckoning of dates, carries little force; for even if in the eleventh book of epigrams which I cited there occur some poems written under the third consulship of either Nerva or Trajan, it does not follow that all the rest were composed at the same time. And although the poet died under Trajan, one must nevertheless suppose that he passed away an old man, and that he had earlier been on familiar terms with Silius Italicus, the writer of the Second Punic War.
