## Reading

...and presbyters and deacons, he returned again to Rome in the twelfth year of Caesar Nero.”

Jerome affirms that Saint Paul, the teacher of the Gentiles—τὸν τὴν οἰκουμένην σταδεύσαντα, καὶ τῷ περὶ πίστεως δρόμῳ τὸν κόσμον μικρὸν ἀποφήναντα [who traversed the whole inhabited world, and by the race he ran for the faith showed the world to be small], as Basil of Seleucia says of him—“ran from ocean to ocean,” imitating his Lord, the Sun of righteousness, of whom we read: “His going forth is from the end of heaven, and his circuit to the ends of it,” so that the earth would fail him sooner than his zeal for preaching. Jerome adds that, after his first defense—which Paul himself mentions in his second epistle to Timothy—and after being released by Nero, the apostle preached the gospel of Christ in the regions of the West as well. The apostle’s own words are: “In my first defense no one stood with me, but everyone deserted me. May it not be charged against them. But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me, so that through me the proclamation might be fulfilled, and all the Gentiles might hear.”

On this passage Theodoret comments: “When, having made his appeal, he was sent to Rome by Festus, and his defense had been heard, he was acquitted; and he set out for Spain, and, hastening on to still other peoples, brought them the light of doctrine.” And, as the same author adds elsewhere, “he brought benefit to the islands that lie in the sea.” Hence Venantius Fortunatus says of him: He crossed the Ocean, even where the island forms a harbour, and the lands the Briton holds, and those that farthest Thule holds.

And Sophronius, patriarch of Jerusalem, in his sermon on the nativity of the apostles, indicates that Paul preached the gospel to the Spaniards and the Britons—which, however, I report on the authority of others
