## Reading

From the Hebrews he (Postellus) tried to trace the origin of the Irish. But even Origen, whom they cite as their authority, plainly indicates the opposite—that "the land of Britain, before the coming of Christ," had by no means assented "to the religion of the one God." Indeed, Dio Cassius records by name that Andraste, or Andate, the goddess of Victory, was worshipped by the Britons, while others report that they regularly worshipped Apollo and Diana as well. And concerning their idol-madness Gildas, a fellow Briton, gives testimony so clear that the matter ought not to be called into question. For he writes thus: "Therefore, passing over those ancient errors, shared with all peoples, by which the whole human race was held fast bound before the coming of Christ in the flesh; and not cataloguing the diabolical monstrosities of my homeland, which almost surpass in number those of Egypt, some of which—with their still-misshapen features, within or outside the deserted walls—we behold even now, stiff in their familiar way, glaring with grim faces; nor crying out by name against the very mountains, or hills, or rivers, once deadly but now useful for human purposes, on which a people then blind heaped divine honour, and so forth."

And so, concerning the most wretched condition of those former times and the most happy condition of the times that followed, blessed Jerome did not hesitate to declare, in the epitaph of Nepotian: "Where then were the men of the whole world, from India even to Britain, from the frozen region of the north even to the heats of the Atlantic ocean—peoples beyond number, and the multitudes of so many nations? How varied in their tongues, in the fashion of their dress, and in their arms!

They were ground down like fish and locusts, like flies and gnats; for without knowledge of his Creator, every man is a beast. But now both the voices and the writings of all nations resound with the passion of Christ and his resurrection."
